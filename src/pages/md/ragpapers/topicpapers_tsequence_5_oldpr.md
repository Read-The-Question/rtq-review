<div class='question question'>

This sequence of numbers goes up by $30$ each time.

$30,\:\:\:\: 60,\:\:\:\: 90,\:\:\:\: 120,\:\:\:\: 150,\:\:\:\: …….$

The sequence continues.

Will the number $1330$ be in the sequence? 

Explain how you know:

</div>
<div class='workings'>
<div class='working'>

As per the question,

The difference between the terms is $30$. Hence, the sequence would be multiples of $30$.

As the sequence goes on increasing in the multiples of 30,

For 1330 to be in the sequence, it should be a multiple of 30.

$
\begin{aligned}
&= \dfrac {1330} {30} \\\\
&= \dfrac {133 \cancel {0}} {3 \cancel {0}} \\\\
&= \dfrac {133} {3} \\\\
&= 44 \dfrac {1} {3} \\\\
\end{aligned}
$

$44 \dfrac {1} {3}$ is not a whole number, as such $1330$ is not a multiple of 30. Hence, the number 1330 will not be in the sequence.

</div>
</div>
<div class='answers'>
<div class='answer'>

No

</div>
</div>

<div class='question subquestion'>

A sequence follows the rule:
Treble and add one
Explain why if the first term is $\frac{-2} {9}$ the second term is $\frac{1} {3}$

</div>
<div class='workings'>
<div class='working'>

As per the question,

The next number is tripled and added by one.

$
\begin{aligned}
&= \dfrac {-2} {9} \times 3 + 1 \\\\
&= \dfrac {-2 \times 3} {9} + 1 \\\\
&= \dfrac {-6} {9} + \dfrac {1 \times 9} {1 \times 9} \\\\
&= \dfrac {-6} {9} + \dfrac {9} {9} \\\\
&= \dfrac {-6 + 9} {9} \\\\
&= \dfrac {3} {9} \\\\
&= \dfrac {3} {3 \times 3} \\\\
&= \dfrac {\cancel {3}} {\cancel {3} \times 3} \\\\
&= \dfrac {1} {3}
\end{aligned}
$

The second term = $\dfrac {1} {3}$

</div>
</div>
<div class='answers'>
<div class='answer'>

$\dfrac {1} {3}$

</div>
</div>

                    &= 1 + 1 \\\\

Using the above solution, we get

$
\begin{aligned}
