If two numbers multiply to give $36$ and their sum is $15$, what are the two numbers?

</div>
<div class='workings'>
<div class='working'>

Let the two numbers $= a$ and $b$

As per the question,

$
\begin{aligned}
a \times b          &= 36 \\\\
a + b               &= 15
\end{aligned}
$

We can easily infer the values by using trial and error.

Lets find out $2$ numbers which meets the following critiera based on the equations above.

- Both the numbers should be smaller than $15$.

- The product of the $2$ numbers is $36$.

- The sum of the $2$ numbers is $15$.

Factors of $36 = 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36$

Factor pairs of $36$ whose sum is less than $15 = (3,12) ,(9,4)$ 

| Numbers   |  Multiplication  |  Sum  |
|:-------:  |:---------------: |:----: |
| $9,  4$   | $36$             | $13$  |
| $3, 12$   | $36$             | $15$  |

A quick check shows that $3$ and $12$ meets all the criteria above.

*Note: The two equations form a quadratic equation. ABBR: Solving quadratic equations is beyond the scope and any such equations can be easily inferred by trial and error method.
*

The two numbers $= 3, 12$

</div>
</div>
<div class='answers'>
<div class='answer'>

$3, 12$

<div class='question subquestion'>

If Jamie makes $30$ cookies, how much plain chocolate will he need?

</div>
<div class='workings'>
<div class='working'>

Let the amount of chocolate needed $= a$

Using equivalent ratio,

$
\begin{aligned}
12 \ \text{cookies} : 300 \ \text{grams}        &= 30 \ \text{cookies} : a \ \text{grams} \\\\
\dfrac{12} {300}                                &= \dfrac {30} {a} \\\\
12 \times a                                     &= 30 \times 300 \\\\
a                                               &= \dfrac {30 \times 300} {12} \\\\
a                                               &= \dfrac {30 \times 12 \times 25} {12} \\\\
a                                               &= \dfrac {30 \times \cancel {12} \times 25} {\cancel {12}} \\\\
a                                               &= 30 \times 25 \\\\
a                                               &= 750 \ \text{g}
\end{aligned}
$

Chocolate needed $= 750 \ \text{g}$

</div>
<div class='working'>

Using ratio,

$
\begin{array}{cccccccccccccc}
  & \text{cookies}                          &:& \text{chocolate} \\\\
= & 12                                      &:& 300 \ \text{g} \\\\
= & 1                                       &:& \dfrac {300} {12} \\\\
= & 1 \times 30                             &:& \dfrac {300 \times 30} {12} \\\\
= & 30                                      &:& \dfrac {12 \times 25 \times 30} {12} \\\\
= & 30                                      &:& \dfrac {\cancel {12} \times 25 \times 30} {\cancel {12}} \\\\
= & 30                                      &:& 25 \times 30 \\\\
= & 30                                      &:& 750 \ \text{g}
\end{array}
$

Chocolate needed $= 750 \ \text{g}$

Kristina bought a bag of sweets and ate $\dfrac{3}{5}$  of them. If she ate $18$ sweets, how many sweets were left over?

</div>
<div class='workings'>
<div class='working'>

Let the number of sweets $= a$

As per the question,

$
\begin{aligned}
\dfrac{3}{5} \times a &= 18 \\\\
a                     &= \dfrac{18 \times 5}{3} \\\\
a                     &= \dfrac{6 \times 3 \times 5}{3} \\\\
a                     &= \dfrac{6 \times \cancel 3 \times 5}{\cancel 3} \\\\
a                     &= 30
\end{aligned}
$

$
\begin{aligned}
