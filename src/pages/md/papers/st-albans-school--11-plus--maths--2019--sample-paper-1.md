<div class='question question'>

Fill in three missing numbers to make these four fractions equivalent.

$\dfrac {4} {\phantom{77}} = \dfrac {\phantom{77}} {10} = \dfrac {12} {15} = \dfrac {20} {\phantom{77}}$

</div>
<div class='workings'>
<div class='working'>

Let the missing numbers $= a, b, c$

As per the question,

$
\begin{aligned}
\dfrac {4} {a}      &= \dfrac {12} {15} \\\\
a                   &= \dfrac {15 \times 4} {12} \\\\
a                   &= \dfrac {5 \times 3 \times 4} {4 \times 3} \\\\
a                   &= \dfrac {5 \times \cancel {3} \times \cancel {4}} {\cancel {4} \times \cancel {3}} \\\\
a                   &= 5 \\\\
\dfrac {b} {10}     &= \dfrac {12} {15} \\\\
b                   &= \dfrac {12 \times 10} {15} \\\\
b                   &= \dfrac {4 \times 3 \times 5 \times 2} {5 \times 3} \\\\
b                   &= \dfrac {4 \times \cancel {3} \times \cancel {5} \times 2} {\cancel {5} \times \cancel {3}} \\\\
b                   &= 4 \times 2 \\\\
b                   &= 8 \\\\
\dfrac {20} {c}     &= \dfrac {12} {15} \\\\
c                   &= \dfrac {15 \times 20} {12} \\\\
c                   &= \dfrac {5 \times 3 \times 4 \times 5} {4 \times 3} \\\\
c                   &= \dfrac {5 \times \cancel {3} \times \cancel {4} \times 5} {\cancel {4} \times \cancel {3}} \\\\
c                   &= 5 \times 5 \\\\
c                   &= 25
\end{aligned}
$

</div>
</div>

<div class='answer'>

<div class='question question'>

Two of the angles in a quardilateral are right angles. One of the others is five times larger than the fourth angle.

What is the size of the largest angle?

</div>
<div class='workings'>
<div class='working'>

ABBR: $\text{Sum of angles in a quadrilateral} = 360 \degree$


Let the third angle $= a$

Fourth angle $= 5a$

As per the question,

$
\begin{aligned}
90 + 90 + a + 5a            &= 360 \\\\
180 + 6a                    &= 360 \\\\
6a                          &= 360 - 180 \\\\
6a                          &= 180 \\\\
a                           &= \dfrac {180} {6} \\\\
a                           &= \dfrac {6 \times 30} {6} \\\\
a                           &= \dfrac {\cancel {6} \times 30} {\cancel {6}} \\\\
a                           &= 30 \degree \\\\
\text {Largest angle}       &= 5a \\\\
                            &= 5 \times 30 \\\\ 
                            &= 150 \degree
\end{aligned}

<div class='question question'>

'Yummy' chocolate bars are packed in either boxes of $5$ or boxes of $12$.

What is the smallest number of full boxes required to pack exactly $2019$ 'Yummy' bars?

</div>
<div class='workings'>
<div class='working'>

Let number of $5$ size boxes $= a$

Let number of $12$ size boxes $= b$

As per the question,

$
\begin{aligned}
5a + 12b              &= 2019 \\\\
5a                    &= 2019 - 12b \\\\
a                     &= \dfrac {2019 - 12b} {5}
\end{aligned}
$

For minimum number of boxes we need to have maximum number of $12$ size boxes.

$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Maximum number of 12 size boxes}    &= \dfrac {2019} {12} \\\\
                                          &= 168 \text{ remainder } 3 \\\\
                                          &\approx 168
\end{aligned}
$

We can easily infer the value of a and b by trial and error.

|  Value of b  |      Value of $12b$       |  Value of $2019 - 12b$  |      Value a           |  Is value of a integer  |
|:------------:|:-------------------------:|:-----------------------:|:----------------------:|:-----------------------:|
|    $168$     |  $12 \times 168 = 2016$   |    $2019 - 2016 = 3$    |  $\dfrac {3} {5}$      |          No             |
|    $167$     |  $12 \times 167 = 2004$   |    $2019 - 2004 = 15$   |  $\dfrac {15} {5} = 3$ |          Yes            |

$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Minimum number of boxes required}   &= a + b \\\\
                                          &= 3 + 167 \\\\
                                          &= 170
\end{aligned}
$

</div>
</div>
