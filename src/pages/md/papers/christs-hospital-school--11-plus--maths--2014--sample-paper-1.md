A cup contains $250 \ \text{millimetres}$. Roughly how many $\ \text{litres}$ is this?

Put a ring round the best answer.

$\text{One-quarter}  \qquad 2.5  \qquad 25$

$

250 \ \text {millilitres}          &= \dfrac {250} {250 \times 4} \ \text {litres} \\\\

250 \ \text {millilitres}          &= \dfrac {1} {4} \ \text {litres} \\\\
250 \ \text {millilitres}          &= 0.250 \ \text {litres}
\end {aligned}
$

$0.250 \ \text {litres}$ is One quarter of a litre.

$\text {One-quarter}$

**Bacteria**

Some bacteria are growing in a dish in a laboratory.

Each day the number of bacteria doubles.

On February $1 \text{st}$ there are $200$ bacteria.

On what day does the number of bacteria first become more than one million?

</div>
<div class='workings'>
<div class='working'>

Let the number of days $= a$

As per the question,

$
\begin{aligned}
2 ^ a \times 200            &> 1000000 \\\\
2 ^ a                       &> \dfrac {1000000} {200} \\\\
2 ^ a                       &> \dfrac {200 \times 5000} {200} \\\\
2 ^ a                       &> \dfrac {\cancel {200} \times 5000} {\cancel {200}} \\\\
2 ^ a                       &> 5000
\end{aligned}
$

We can easily infer the value by using trial and error. Lets find out the value of $a$ that satisfies the above equation.

|  Value of a  |  Value of $2 ^ a$  |  Value $> 5000$    |
|:------------:|:------------------:|:------------------:|
|     $10$     |        $1024$      |    $\text {No}$    |
|     $11$     |        $2048$      |    $\text {No}$    |
|     $12$     |        $4096$      |    $\text {No}$    |
|     $13$     |        $8192$      |    $\text {Yes}$   |

$
\begin{aligned}
\text {The day when number of bacteria become more than one million} &= 1 \ \text{February} + 13 \ \text{days} \\\\
                                                                     &= 14 \ \text {February}
\end{aligned}
$

</div>
</div>
<div class='answers'>
<div class='answer'>

$14 \ \text{February}$

</div>

**Number pattern**

Here is the first part of a number pattern:

$13,  \quad 16,  \quad 19,  \quad 22,  \quad 25,  \quad \ldots$

Which of these numbers will appear somewhere in the pattern?

$36,  \quad 46,  \quad 56,  \quad 100,  \quad 1000$

</div>
<div class='workings'>
<div class='working'>

ABBR:  Let's find the relation between the consecutive terms of the sequence.


The number is calculated by adding $3$ to the previous number.

This is an arithmetic sequence too and the difference between the consecutive patterns of the sequence is constant.

The next number is calculated by adding multiple of $2$ to the first number and the number of times it is added is based on the position in the sequence.

Let's check with all these numbers

Let the term number $= a$

$
\begin{aligned}
13 + (a - 1) \times 3   &=  36 \\\\
13 + 3a - 3             &=  36 \\\\
3a + 10                 &=  36 \\\\
3a                      &=  36 - 10 \\\\
3a                      &=  26 \\\\
a                       &=  \dfrac{26}{3} \\\\
a                       &=  8.66 \text{ (Not possible)}
\end{aligned}
$

$
\begin{aligned}
13 + (a - 1) \times 3   &=  46 \\\\
13 + 3a - 3             &=  46 \\\\
3a + 10                 &=  46 \\\\
3a                      &=  46 - 10 \\\\
3a                      &=  36 \\\\
a                       &=  \dfrac{36}{3} \\\\
a                       &=  \dfrac{12 \times 3}{3} \\\\
a                       &=  \dfrac{12 \times \cancel{3}}{\cancel{3}} \\\\
a                       &=  12 \text{ (possible)}
\end{aligned}
$

$
\begin{aligned}
13 + (a - 1) \times 3   &=  56 \\\\
13 + 3a - 3             &=  56 \\\\
3a + 10                 &=  56 \\\\
3a                      &=  56 - 10 \\\\
3a                      &=  46 \\\\
a                       &=  \dfrac{46}{3} \\\\
a                       &=  15.33 \text{ (Not possible)}
\end{aligned}
$

$
\begin{aligned}
13 + (a - 1) \times 3   &=  100 \\\\
13 + 3a - 3             &=  100 \\\\
3a + 10                 &=  100 \\\\
3a                      &=  100 - 10 \\\\
3a                      &=  90 \\\\
a                       &=  \dfrac{90}{3} \\\\
a                       &=  \dfrac{30 \times 3}{3} \\\\
a                       &=  \dfrac{30 \times \cancel{3}}{\cancel{3}} \\\\
a                       &=  30 \text{ (possible)}
\end{aligned}
$

$
\begin{aligned}
13 + (a - 1) \times 3   &=  1000 \\\\
13 + 3a - 3             &=  1000 \\\\
3a + 10                 &=  1000 \\\\
3a                      &=  1000 - 10 \\\\
3a                      &=  990 \\\\
a                       &=  \dfrac{990}{3} \\\\
a                       &=  \dfrac{330 \times 3}{3} \\\\
a                       &=  \dfrac{330 \times \cancel{3}}{\cancel{3}} \\\\
a                       &=  330 \text{ (possible)}
\end{aligned}
$

The numbers will appear are $46$,$100$,$1000$

</div>
</div>

<div class='answer'>

$46$,$100$,$1000$

</div>
